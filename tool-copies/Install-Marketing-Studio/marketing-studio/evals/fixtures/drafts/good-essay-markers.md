Picture a design review on a Thursday afternoon.

Six people sit around a prototype that was built in a morning. The layout is clean and the copy reads well. The flow works on the first try. Someone says "this looks good", and nobody disagrees, because it does.

The review ends twenty minutes early. Everyone leaves happy, and nobody has decided anything.

I keep coming back to that room when I think about where product work is heading. Nothing in it went wrong. A good-enough result arrived quickly, and nobody had a reason to question it. Multiply that by every team, every week, and you get a very large number of products that are fine.

This essay is about what happens to the people who build those products when fine becomes free, and about the one thing I have found that does not get cheaper.

## Good enough is now free

For most of my career, good enough had a price. You paid it in weeks of work: a brief, a few rounds of exploration, a review, a build, a round of fixes. The price filtered things. A team that could not afford to reach good enough did not ship, and a team that could was already ahead of most of the market.

That filter has gone. A first version that used to need a team now needs an afternoon and a clear sentence. Anyone can ask for an app, a landing page or a component, and the result will be competent. The spacing will be even and the labels sensible, and the colours will go together.

Competent is the problem, because competent is also what everyone else receives. These tools learned from what has already been published, so when you give them nothing of your own, they return what is most common. The same request from a thousand people produces roughly the same answer a thousand times, and each of those people believes they made something.

Nobody sets out to look like everyone else. Each team looks sideways, sees what is working, and does the same. With these tools the sideways glance takes seconds, and the average arrives for everyone at once.

I do not think this is a reason to panic, and I do not think it is a reason to stop using the tools. It is a reason to be precise about where the value moved.

When good enough was expensive, the value was in getting there. Now that it is free, the value is in everything the tool could not have known: who the product is for, what they were doing before they opened it, what the company has already decided, and what it refuses to do. None of that is on the internet. It lives in people, in documents nobody has opened since the kickoff, and in the memory of the one colleague who remembers why the button is where it is.

Which is why the question for a design review has changed. It used to be "is this good?" Now it has to be "who decided this?"

## What caring looks like in the work

Caring sounds soft, and I want to make it concrete, because otherwise it becomes a slogan, and a slogan is easy to agree with and hard to act on.

When I say a team cares, I mean you can see it in specific places. Here are four I look for.

→ Someone went and asked the people who use the product, before opening any tool
→ The context the machine needs is written down where the machine can read it
→ The first idea was treated as a starting point, and alternatives were compared
→ Someone polished the parts people will actually feel, and left the rest alone

None of these is glamorous. None of them shows up in a screenshot. A reviewer looking at the finished product could not tell whether any of them happened, and that is exactly why they get skipped. When the result already looks finished, the pressure to keep going disappears.

The first one matters most to me, and it is the hardest for people who build products for a living to hear. Someone who lives a problem every day knows things about it that we will never find in a brief. If we do not go and get that knowledge, the person who has it can now build the product without us. That is uncomfortable, and I include myself. I would rather feel the discomfort now than discover it later.

The second one is about the machine. An agent fills every gap you leave with a confident guess. If the brand, the system and the decisions you have already made are not written down, then for the machine they do not exist, and it will invent replacements that look plausible. A team that cares writes the context down, because it wants the guess to be right.

The third and fourth are about time. AI makes one version cheap, which also makes ten versions cheap. The honest use of that saving is to look further and choose better. The dishonest use is to deliver the same thing faster and call it productivity. Both look identical in a status report. Only one of them is a product that people remember.

Imagine the Thursday review again, with one difference. Next to each decision there is a name. The spacing follows the system, and a person chose the exception on the checkout page, with a reason attached. The review takes the same twenty minutes. But now there is something to discuss, and something to learn from the next time.

> [PULL QUOTE] When good enough is free, the decision is the part of the work that is still yours.

I want to be careful here. I am not saying caring means working longer, and I am not saying it means distrusting AI. A team that cares can finish early. What it cannot do is finish without anyone having decided anything.

## My own numbers, and where to put the time

My LinkedIn profile gathered 857,763 impressions in 13 months, most of them from people outside my network.[^1] I am not a LinkedIn expert, and I said so when I published my toolkit. I kept testing things and writing down what happened, then cleaned the list up and shared it as a guide.

A number like that says little about quality. What interests me is what it is made of.

I did not plan my niche on the first day. I could have. Every guide tells you to pick one, and I would have picked something that sounded sensible. What actually happened is that a job post for design roles got unusually high engagement, and that told me something about my audience that I would never have guessed. They cared about the design community. So I wrote more for them.

That was caring in a small form. The evidence was there for anyone who looked. Most people do not look, because the tools make it easy to produce the next post without reading what happened to the last one. Caring, in that case, was reading the results, writing them down, and letting them change what I did next.

Caring also costs less than people expect. It does not need a bigger team or a bigger budget, and it does not need a new process. It needs someone who reads what happened and writes it down, and a habit that survives a busy week.

You can do the same with a product. Look at what actually happened after you shipped. Which part did people use, and where did they stop? What did they ask about that you thought was obvious? Those answers are the context your next brief should start from, and the machine cannot collect them on its own.

There is a second thing the numbers taught me. The people who read the guide did not need another opinion about LinkedIn. There are plenty of those. They needed a list that came from something somebody had tried. The value was in the testing, and the testing was slow, and it was mine. Nobody could have copied it by asking a tool for a guide, because the tool had not run the tests.

I think this holds well beyond my own profile. When anyone can produce a competent answer, the answer stops being the thing people pay attention to. They pay attention to the person who can tell them why it is right for their situation, and who has the evidence to back it up.

All of this ends in a practical question about time. If you lead a team, it is what to do with the hours that AI hands back.

There are two options, and they feel the same on the day. You can deliver more, or you can deliver better. If all the saved time goes into volume, you get more products that look like everyone else's, produced faster. If some of it goes back into the parts that need judgment, you get fewer surprises and a product with a recognisable point of view.

Someone has to make that choice on purpose. It will not happen by default, because the default is to fill the calendar. I would write it into the plan: a share of the time that AI saves is spent on talking to users, writing context down, comparing alternatives and polishing the parts people feel. If it is not in the plan, it will be the first thing to go, because it never looks urgent. Protecting that time is a leadership decision, and a small one. It costs a line in the plan and a conversation with whoever owns the deadline.

If you are building your own career, the same logic holds. The tools will keep getting better at the competent part. What will matter is whether you can say why a decision was made, and whether you have a record of what you tested. That is something you can start today, with no tool at all. A notebook is enough, and so is a shared document that your team actually opens. The format matters much less than the habit of coming back to it.

And if you are hiring, look for it. In a portfolio, the finished work tells you little, since everything finished looks good now. Ask about a decision that went against the first idea. Ask what the person changed after they saw how it landed. People who care have those stories ready, because they were paying attention when it happened.

I will end with something practical. This week, pick one thing you shipped that nobody questioned. Find the decision inside it, the one that looked too small to discuss. Write down who made it and why. If you cannot answer, you have just found the place where care was missing, and it is an easy place to start.
